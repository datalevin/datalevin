## Abstract

Flexible entity-attribute-value (EAV) stores simplify data modeling but make
cost-based optimization uncertain: relational histograms do not naturally
capture entity-centered predicates, correlations, or directional graph
fanout. Query-time sampling can recover this information, but unlucky samples
can cross a plan-selection boundary and produce catastrophic execution-time
tails. We present Datalevin's tail-robust optimizer for durable Datalog.
Datalevin first removes avoidable uncertainty using count and rank operations
over nested EAV/AVE indexes. It then samples the concrete index ranges and
directions named by the query, and manages noisy fanout observations using a
storage-derived prior. These estimates drive entity-centered merge scans,
indexed directional joins,
bitmap-filtered hash joins, and semi-joins. On a 277.9-million-triple
translation of the Join Order Benchmark, across 30 deterministic sample seeds
(3,390 query-seed trials per policy),
raw sampling produced 48 catastrophic executions and no timeouts, whereas
storage-prior shrinkage produced eight and the production envelope produced
four. Shrinkage reduced P95 and P99 slowdown from 2.86× and 12.26× to 1.45×
and 2.64×, respectively. In 10 randomized runtime schedules, disabling
query-local sampling made the same eight queries time out in every repetition
(80 timeouts), versus none for the complete system. The resulting
cardinality-estimation pipeline uses exact information where available and
explicitly manages uncertainty where estimation remains.

## 1. Introduction

Datalog systems pair a compact declarative language with a flexible data model.
In EAV-oriented systems, facts are triples `(entity, attribute, value)` rather
than fields in fixed-width rows. Applications can add attributes without table
migrations, compose queries from data patterns and rules, and traverse
relationships in either direction. These properties make Datalog a useful
application query language and a promising target for generated queries.

The same representation creates an optimizer problem. A logical object that
would occupy one relational row is spread across several triples. A query over
several object types initially appears to contain many self-joins, while
foreign-key-like relationships are interleaved with ordinary values.

The optimizer must estimate selective attribute predicates, correlations among
attributes of one entity, and fanout across relationships. Relational
histograms attach distributions to typed columns, but EAV places every
attribute in the same value position: a histogram over `value` mixes domains
such as years, names, and entity IDs. A separate histogram per attribute
recovers only a marginal distribution. It cannot estimate whether two
predicates hold on the same entity or the fanout of a relationship conditioned
on entities already selected. Covering those cases requires multiattribute and
join statistics over open-ended attribute combinations and traversal
directions. Such statistics are expensive to maintain and can lag a changing
durable database.

The optimizer's objective is not merely a good estimate or plan on average.
Plan selection is discontinuous: a modest cardinality perturbation can change
the starting relation, join direction, or physical operator, after which
intermediate results may grow by orders of magnitude. Sampling therefore
creates a second problem while addressing the first. Most samples may select
the same good plan, yet a rare sample can cause a severe execution-time
outlier. A durable system needs predictable planning behavior across those
samples, not only a favorable mean.

Datalevin addresses these uncertainties in layers. Its nested EAV and AVE
indexes are ordered and countable. Point and range operations remove uncertainty
about marginal cardinalities for the current database snapshot. Where counts
cannot reveal entity-level correlation or relationship fanout, the planner
samples the exact index ranges and physical directions named by the query.
Finally, it treats the resulting sample as uncertain evidence: a
storage-derived average supplies a prior target, while sample size and
dispersion control how aggressively a shrinkage policy trusts the local
observation. A separate conservative policy uses the larger of the raw
observation and prior.

Before enumerating plans, Datalevin recognizes all attribute patterns centered
on one entity variable and compiles them into a single merge scan over the EAV
index. The optimizer therefore plans over a smaller graph of entity stars and
relationship edges, not over every triple pattern independently. It enumerates
connected left-deep plans, estimates relationship directions separately, and
chooses physical operators that exploit the same indexes used for estimation.

This paper makes three contributions:

1. It presents a layered architecture for managing cardinality uncertainty in
   Datalog planning: countable indexes resolve base cardinalities, while
   query-local directional samples estimate correlation and fanout that counts
   cannot observe.
2. It presents lightweight storage-aware uncertainty policies—empirical-Bayes
   shrinkage and a conservative prior envelope—designed to reduce the risk
   that noisy query-local samples select catastrophic plans, without an
   offline learned model or conventional histograms.
3. It experimentally separates the effects of counts, sampling, and sampling
   uncertainty management using paired deterministic samples on all 113 JOB
   queries over 277.9 million triples, reporting plan stability and
   execution-time tails rather than only mean performance.

Datalevin does retain lightweight background metadata, including
attribute-level average fanout, as a shrinkage target and fallback. The claim
is not that the optimizer is statistics-free or that sampling is always
accurate. It is that a durable store can derive cardinality information in
stages of increasing uncertainty—counts, samples, then regularized
estimates—and thereby reduce the long execution-time tail caused by
occasionally misleading samples.

## 2. Managing Cardinality Uncertainty

### 2.1 Nested ordered indexes

A literal triple index repeats long prefixes. In EAV order, every attribute and
value repeats its entity; in AVE order, an attribute and value prefix may occur
with many entities. Datalevin builds on LMDB's `DUPSORT` facility, which maps
one key to an ordered duplicate list. The result behaves as a nested B+ tree:
the outer tree stores a shared prefix and the inner tree stores the remaining
components. Page-prefix compression in the underlying storage layer removes
further redundancy. We use this representation for its storage locality and
for the count and rank operations it exposes to query planning; storage-space
reduction is not an empirical claim of this paper.

The layout also exposes the quantity an optimizer needs most. For an AVE point
predicate `[?e :a v]`, the number of matching entities is the duplicate count
at key `(:a, v)`, available in constant time. For an interval predicate, such
as a production year between 2005 and 2010, rank metadata in the ordered index
provides the range cardinality in logarithmic time. EAV counts handle a known
entity and attribute. Counts are capped when the optimizer only needs to know
which predicate is smallest, limiting unnecessary work.

Unlike a periodically refreshed histogram, an index count describes the
database snapshot being queried. This matters in a durable system with
continuous updates: there is no window in which the data and its primary
selectivity evidence disagree. Datalevin nevertheless validates a reported
zero against the index before treating the entire query as empty. This
defensive check reflects a useful implementation principle: an inaccurate
estimate may produce a slow plan, whereas a false zero changes the answer.

### 2.2 Predicate pushdown and base cardinalities

The query compiler rewrites single-variable inequalities into index bounds and
pushes eligible functions into attribute scans. It also substitutes constant
inputs before planning. Consequently, the count and the later scan describe
the same restricted key range. This tight correspondence avoids a common
source of estimator mismatch in which statistics describe a column but
execution applies a more complex predicate.

For each entity star, the optimizer counts its bound and free attribute
clauses, chooses the most selective path as the initial scan, and records counts
for the alternatives. Small base relations can be executed during planning;
large ones are represented by a bounded sample. This thresholded policy keeps
planning work proportional to the evidence required rather than the size of
the database.

### 2.3 Query-local sampling

Counts establish marginal selectivity, but do not reveal whether several
attributes co-occur on the same entities or how many target entities a
relationship reaches. Datalevin samples from the concrete ranges identified
for the current query. A sample of entity IDs is passed through the same merge
scan and predicates that execution will use. The surviving fraction estimates
the selectivity of the complete star, including local correlations.

Relationship estimates are directional. Suppose the current plan contains
entities `A`, and a reference attribute links `A` to `B`. Expanding stored
forward references, looking up reverse references, and joining equal values
can have very different fanouts even though they correspond to the same
logical edge. The estimator therefore samples the actual direction and
physical access path under consideration. For each sampled input entity it
records the number of linked outputs, retaining the sample size, mean,
dispersion, and maximum observed fanout.

Sampling supplies evidence that counts cannot, but a raw sample mean is not a
safe planning policy. Rare heavy hitters may be absent or overrepresented, and
estimation errors across later joins remain dependent. More importantly, plan
choice is sensitive to which side of a cost crossover the sample happens to
place an estimate. Our evaluation therefore treats raw sampling as an explicit
ablation rather than assuming that adding samples monotonically improves plan
quality.

### 2.4 Managing sampling uncertainty

Datalevin regularizes a sampled directional fanout toward the attribute's
storage-derived average fanout. For sample mean \(\bar{x}\), sample size \(n\),
and prior mean \(\mu_0\), the center of the estimate is

\[
\tilde{\mu} =
\frac{n\bar{x} + k_{\mathrm{eff}}\mu_0}
     {n + k_{\mathrm{eff}}},
\qquad
k_{\mathrm{eff}} = k(1 + \alpha\,\mathrm{CV}^2).
\]

Thus, larger and more stable samples receive more weight, while dispersion
moves the estimate toward the durable fallback. We also evaluated an additive
tail term that treats the largest observed fanout as one-sided
pseudo-evidence. Given a center \(\mu_c\) with evidence weight \(m\), it
computes

\[
\Delta_\tau =
\frac{\tau}{m+\tau}\max(0, x_{\max}-\mu_c),
\qquad
\hat{\mu} = \mu_c + \Delta_\tau.
\]

For the raw center, \(m=n\). For the shrinkage center,
\(m=n+k_{\mathrm{eff}}\). The tail pseudo-count \(\tau\) therefore controls a
bounded adjustment toward an observed heavy fanout without multiplying the
entire estimate or pulling the center downward. Our development sweep found
that positive values did not improve the execution tail and that a large value
created catastrophic plans, so production fixes \(\tau=0\). We retain the
formula as a reported negative design result, not as a claimed mechanism.

The current production policy applies an explicit conservative floor at the
larger of the raw sample mean and storage-derived fanout, followed by the
semantic minimum. At \(\tau=0\), this floor always dominates the shrinkage
center because the latter lies between the sample mean and prior. Production
is therefore a conservative envelope, not “shrinkage plus a floor.” We
evaluate shrinkage and the envelope as distinct policies.

The contribution is not shrinkage as a statistical primitive. It is the use of
storage-derived prior evidence and observed sample dispersion to stabilize
physical plan selection. The empirical claim is correspondingly operational:
we test whether shrinkage and the conservative envelope, compared with raw
sampling on the same sampled ranks, reduce plan flips, high-percentile
slowdown, and observed catastrophic plans. Section 4 reports the rejected tail
term separately before attributing an effect to either supported mechanism.

The tail pseudo-count remains part of the named experimental condition. A
value of zero is frozen for the disjoint final run; positive candidates were
evaluated only on development seeds. Each observation records the no-tail
center, tail adjustment, conservative floor, and final estimate so that the
negative result is auditable.

## 3. Simplifying and Planning the Query Graph

### 3.1 Entity stars and merge scans

Several patterns with the same entity variable are not independent relations
in the physical store. For example, fetching a title's name, year, and kind
means locating candidate title IDs and then reading several adjacent
attributes. Datalevin groups these patterns into an entity star. Its merge-scan
operator walks candidate entity IDs in order and scans the requested
attributes in attribute order, applying predicates as values are encountered.
This resembles a pivot scan: it produces a relation for the logical object in
one coordinated EAV walk instead of materializing and joining one relation per
attribute.

Collapsing stars changes both execution and optimization. A query with dozens
of triple patterns may become a small graph whose nodes are entity types and
whose edges are references or shared values. The optimizer no longer
enumerates permutations of joins that merge-scan execution will never
materialize. It instead chooses a starting star and an order and direction for
crossing the remaining graph edges.

This normalization is architectural context, not a novelty claim. Pivot scans,
characteristic sets, and structure-aware RDF planning establish closely related
ideas [2, 4, 11, 13]. We describe Datalevin's version because it defines the
units whose cardinalities are estimated and the physical paths whose
directional costs are compared.

### 3.2 Plan enumeration

Datalevin uses a Selinger-style dynamic program over connected subsets of each
query-graph component. It produces left-deep trees because each indexed
expansion requires a base relation and because star collapse leaves graphs that
are often chain-like. Disconnected components are planned independently,
executed concurrently, and combined in estimated-size order.

Exhaustive dynamic programming is valuable for small graphs but its search
cost can dominate planning for generated or unusually broad queries. The
enumerator exhaustively considers all possible permutations of two entity
stars. A component of \(n\) stars has \(n\) choices for the first star and
\(n-1\) for the second, hence \(n(n-1)\) two-star permutations. This number,
or a lower configurable cap, is the switch threshold. Once a later
dynamic-programming round exceeds it, the planner switches from exhaustive
order enumeration to
greedy representative selection. It groups candidates by unordered star set,
retains the cheapest plan for each set, and discards the other internal orders
before continuing. This heuristic concentrates search effort on the initial
order, which typically has the greatest effect on starting cardinality,
traversal direction, and early intermediate sizes, while bounding the search
over later joins.

The accepted 30-seed run characterizes this mechanism over 13,530 completed
query-policy plans with 4–17 entity stars. This plan-space reduction was
applied to every completed plan. The aggregate candidate-state count had
median 152, P95 2,562, and maximum 13,563; retained states had median 93, P95
1,661, and maximum 13,260. Median end-to-end preparation time was 30.9 ms for
Full, 31.4 ms for Shrinkage, 30.0 ms for Raw, and 35.5 ms for No counts. This
is workload characterization rather than a search-policy ablation.

### 3.3 Physical alternatives

The cost model chooses among physical methods rather than merely permuting
logical joins:

- A forward reference can feed target entity IDs directly into a merge scan.
  A reverse reference probes AVE to recover source entity IDs. Equal ordinary
  values use a related indexed lookup.
- Reverse-reference and value-equality edges may instead use a hash join. A
  bitmap built from the input provides sideways information passing to filter
  the other scan before hashing, reducing both tuples and memory.
- If a degree-one target exists only to test existence and contributes no
  required output, an indexed semi-join emits each input tuple once rather than
  materializing its fanout.
- Datalog-specific `or-join` and selected `not-join` forms receive dedicated
  planning and filtering behavior. Multiple recursive rule branches can expose
  join links after rule resolution, while preserving their declared join
  variables.

Planning and execution are pipelined. Components can be planned in parallel,
and execution batches begin without requiring the entire query result to be
materialized at every boundary.

## 4. Evaluation

We ask five questions. **Q1.** What execution-time tail does the complete
optimizer exhibit across deterministic samples? **Q2.** Do direct index counts
remove material base-cardinality failures? **Q3.** Does query-local sampling improve
plans by exposing correlation and directional fanout, and how often does raw
sampling destabilize them? **Q4.** Do shrinkage and the conservative prior
envelope reduce that sampling-induced execution-time tail? **Q5.** What
planning overhead and parameter sensitivity accompany the robustness gain?

### 4.1 Setup and protocol

We use the Join Order Benchmark, whose 113 queries were designed to expose
cardinality-estimation and join-order failures on a real, highly correlated
IMDB snapshot. We translated its schema and queries to Datalevin Datalog and
manually checked result equivalence with the SQL workload. The Datalevin
database contains 277,878,411 triples and occupies 17 GB.

Experiments run on a November 2023 16-inch MacBook Pro with an Apple M3 Pro
(six performance and six efficiency cores), 36 GB memory, and a 1 TB SSD.
Because LMDB maps database pages into the process address space, concurrent
memory pressure can dominate a timing result. Final runs disable Docker and
other memory-intensive services, record memory pressure, page faults, and swap
deltas, and discard any pass with new swap activity or thermal throttling.
The accepted artifacts record macOS 26.5.1 on AArch64, OpenJDK 21.0.11,
Datalevin 1.0.0 and native library 0.18.3.
The recorded repository revision is
`e85db752897e20da165c1811da90e32e0950b584`; manifests also retain the exact
commands and JVM options.

Each measured pass assigns every query a deterministic sample seed derived
from the pass seed. All estimator policies receive identical sampled ranks for
that query and pass, making comparisons paired and exactly replayable. Queries
receive fresh plan caches, policy order is randomized within a pass, and
warm-up uses a disjoint seed range. We use 30 measured seeds for Full, No
counts, Raw, and Shrinkage. Because No sampling consumes no reservoir
sample, it runs in a separate 10-repetition randomized-schedule experiment;
calling 30 nominal seeds independent estimator replicates would be
pseudoreplication. Every query-policy execution has a fixed 30-second timeout.
Timeouts remain in all denominators and workload totals.

Five development seeds are reserved for debugging, formula selection, and
timeout calibration. Before examining the final results, we freeze the named
estimator formulas, prior parameters, sample budget, timeout, health checks,
and analysis script. The 30 final evaluation seeds are disjoint from these
development seeds.

### 4.3 Sampling-uncertainty decomposition

The observation motivating the paper is that raw query-local sampling
occasionally selects a radically worse plan, while uncertainty management
substantially suppresses this behavior. We test attribution directly with
counts and sampled ranks held fixed:

| Estimator policy | Shrink toward storage prior | Conservative envelope |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| Raw mean |  |  |
| Shrinkage only | ✓ |  |
| Production | masked by envelope | \(\max(\bar{x},\mu_0)\) |

These policies must be implemented as named formulas, not only combinations of
interacting switches. In particular, a conservative lower bound can
mathematically dominate a shrinkage center; the experiment verifies the
selected formula on every recorded sample observation. Positive-tail variants
are reported only as a development sensitivity experiment.

In the accepted five-seed development decomposition, all 2,260 trials
completed with exactly paired sample fingerprints and clean machine-health
intervals. Raw sampling produced eight observed \(\geq10\times\) trials,
versus zero for shrinkage-only and zero for the production envelope. Five raw
failures were query 22d under every seed; its median execution fell from
6.793 s under raw sampling to 0.608 s under shrinkage. The raw/shrink median
ratios were also 5.94x for query 11d and 5.61x for 22c. Across all 565 trials,
raw and shrinkage had P99 slowdowns of 10.386 and 3.428, respectively, and
plan-flip rates of 13.10% and 9.73%.

The production envelope reduced the observed plan-flip rate further to 5.49%,
but telemetry confirmed that its floor dominated the shrinkage center in all
6,923 observations. We therefore attribute the raw-to-shrink result to
shrinkage and treat the envelope as a separate candidate policy. These
development measurements motivated the disjoint-seed confirmatory run below.

The 30-seed confirmatory decomposition establishes the direct contribution.
Raw produced 48 catastrophic trials, of which 47 selected a plan different
from Full; Shrinkage produced eight, of which five were plan-different. Raw's
events included query 22d under all 30 seeds and query 11d under eight, while
Shrinkage reduced 22d to two threshold crossings. Relative to Raw, Shrinkage
reduced P95 slowdown by 1.412 [0.379, 5.511], P99 by 9.620
[2.693, 11.288], and geometric-mean slowdown by 0.115 [0.041, 0.208].
The catastrophe-rate reduction was 1.180 percentage points, although its
query-clustered interval [0.000, 3.187] touches zero. We therefore claim a
large reduction in the continuous and plan-linked tail, not eradication.

The conservative envelope removed both residual Shrinkage catastrophes on
22d, but did not improve the aggregate tail significantly: Full minus
Shrinkage was 0.247 at P99 [-0.690, 1.574] and -0.118 percentage points in
catastrophe rate [-0.442, 0.147]. Its geometric-mean slowdown was 0.050 higher
[0.004, 0.098]. We retain the envelope as a separate query-specific safety
policy rather than crediting it with the primary aggregate result.

The disjoint 100-seed plan census then completed all 45,200 plan-only trials.
Validation found all 11,300 query-seed groups paired, all 138,552 shared
sample fingerprints identical, all 101 pass intervals clean, and the
no-floor/Shrinkage estimator and structural plan exactly equal in all 11,300
controls. Raw deviated from its per-query modal plan in 15.37% of trials,
compared with 11.40% for Shrinkage and 5.79% for the production envelope.

Raw and Shrinkage selected different plans on 6,177 of 11,300 paired
query-seeds (54.66%), spanning 80 queries. They differed under every one of
the 100 seeds for 36 queries, including 11d, 22c, and 22d. The census
therefore establishes broad, reproducible policy disagreement; the
confirmatory execution results above establish its runtime consequences.

For query \(q\), seed \(s\), and policy \(p\), define slowdown relative to the
complete optimizer as

\[
S_{q,s,p} =
\frac{T_{q,s,p}}
     {\operatorname{median}_{s'} T_{q,s',\mathrm{baseline}}}.
\]

The baseline is the explicitly marked Full condition rather than a name
inferred by the analysis script.

Before inspecting the final results, we define a catastrophic execution as a
timeout or \(S_{q,s,p} \ge 10\). We report this count alongside the continuous
slowdown distribution so the conclusion does not depend on one threshold.
When a policy has zero observed catastrophic executions, we state “zero
observed in \(N\) query-seed trials” and report a confidence bound rather than
claiming universal elimination.

Plan stability is measured independently of runtime noise: number of distinct
plan hashes per query, probability of deviating from the modal plan, and the
frequency with which the start node, edge direction, or physical join method
changes across seeds. For each raw-sampling catastrophe we replay the same seed
under all estimator policies and retain the sampled fanouts, estimates, chosen
plan, intermediate cardinalities, and execution trace.

### 4.4 Estimation quality and explanatory cases

Instrumentation records estimated and actual output cardinality at each
materialized plan boundary. For nonzero actual cardinality \(a\) and estimate
\(e\), we report q-error `max(e/a, a/e)`; zero cases are reported in separate
false-empty and false-nonempty categories. Because severe plans are often
caused by underestimation, we preserve the sign of log error rather than
reporting only symmetric q-error. We aggregate median, 90th, 95th, 99th, and
maximum error by base star, directional edge, and complete component.

Our principal explanatory case was selected from the accepted 30-seed
artifact, not from an anecdotal development run. Among clean completed query
22d trials, we selected the slowest Raw execution whose paired Shrinkage and
Full executions completed without worker rewarming: run 20, query sample seed
213459894921913. In the accepted timing run, Raw took 7.087 s, Shrinkage
0.589 s, and Full 0.415 s. An exact replay reproduced the three structural
plan hashes and ordering at 4.952 s, 0.534 s, and 0.179 s, respectively.

The trace exposes the mechanism. For reverse traversal through
`movie-keyword/movie`, the raw estimate was clamped at 1.000, shrinkage raised
it to 7.528, and the production floor to 9.488. Corresponding estimates for
`movie-info-idx/movie` were 1.000, 1.102, and 3.001, and for
`movie-companies/movie` they were 1.000, 1.125, and 2.400. Raw consequently
expanded movie-info-index and movie-company relations before applying the
kind filter. Its largest actual intermediate was 250,351 tuples. Shrinkage
and Full applied the kind filter earlier and peaked at 75,603 tuples, then
used a bitmap-filtered hash join for the final movie-info relation.

Symmetric cardinality error alone would tell the wrong story: maximum stage
q-error was 6.75 for Raw, 6.87 for Shrinkage, and 34.68 for Full. The robust
policies deliberately avoid optimistic estimates at decision boundaries even
when that worsens q-error. Validation confirmed one paired timing group, all
17 shared sample sources with identical fingerprints, all 30 counted plan
stages, and clean timing, warm-up, and cardinality health intervals.

### 4.5 Overhead, sensitivity, and limitations

We report the planning-time cost of counts, sampling, and regularization
separately. After the confirmatory experiments, we ran a frozen exploratory
sweep on all 59 queries whose Raw plan varied in the accepted census plus 20
deterministically selected stable controls. Eight one-factor-at-a-time
conditions varied sample budget \(m\in\{250,1000,4000\}\), prior weight
\(k\in\{0,25,100,400\}\), and variance inflation
\(\alpha\in\{0,0.4,1.6\}\) around the shrink-only setting
\((m=1000,k=100,\alpha=0.4)\). Ten new deterministic seeds produced 6,320
completed trials with no timeout or result mismatch.

| \(m=250\) | 0 | 2.457 | 4.067 | 26.9 |
| \(m=4000\) | 5 | 2.224 | 8.579 | 226.0 |

Removing the prior significantly worsened P95 by 1.121
[0.123, 6.583], P99 by 6.073 [0.089, 10.870], geometric-mean slowdown by
0.101 [0.003, 0.223], and catastrophe rate by 1.139 percentage points
[0.127, 2.911]. Eight of nine \(k=0\) catastrophes changed the structural
plan. In the plan-variable cohort, all seven were plan-different and the P95
and P99 excesses remained significant; the stable controls showed no
significant contrast. Neither stronger regularization setting significantly
dominated the frozen choice. Increasing the budget to 4,000 was not
monotonically safer and increased median planning time 5.8×. We therefore
treat this as support for prior shrinkage's tail effect, not as post-hoc
parameter selection.

A five-seed development sweep over tail pseudo-count
\(\tau\in\{0,1,10,100\}\) selected \(\tau=0\): positive values did not improve
the tail, while \(\tau=100\) introduced 11 catastrophic slowdowns. We therefore
disable the additive tail term for final execution and report the grid as a
negative sensitivity result. Relative to \(\tau=0\), \(\tau=10\) changed
68/565 paired plans and increased P95 slowdown from 1.580 to 1.800.
\(\tau=100\) changed 230/565 plans, raised P95/P99 slowdown to 3.354/14.170,
and increased the plan-flip rate from 5.49% to 16.11%. This determines whether
tail robustness comes from the remaining uncertainty mechanisms rather than
one fortunate sample size or hyperparameter.

The evaluation uses one data model and one complex analytical workload. JOB is
valuable because of its correlations and established use in optimizer
research, but it does not cover updates, recursive rules, or
application-style point queries. Finite deterministic seeds can establish an
observed failure rate, not prove that a blowup is impossible.
The artifact will include raw per-query rows, sample and schedule seeds,
timeouts, plans, estimator observations, database size, load and analysis
time, exact versions, JVM settings, and machine-health logs.

## 5. Related Work

Selinger et al. established dynamic-programming join enumeration and
cost-based access-path selection [14]. Later work improved enumeration for
connected subgraphs and studied the tradeoff among estimation, cost models,
and search [7, 12]. Datalevin adopts this foundation but applies it after
entity-star collapse and changes search policy dynamically when enumeration
exceeds a plan budget.

RDF optimizers exploit characteristic sets and query graph structure to
estimate and order multi-pattern queries [4, 11, 13]. Pivot scans similarly
retrieve multiple attributes without a sequence of self-joins [2].
Datalevin's entity stars and merge scans build on these insights, specialized
to explicit entity IDs, typed reference attributes, and nested EAV storage.

Sampling has long been used for join selectivity [5], including index-based
join sampling evaluated on JOB [9]. Bayesian formulations have combined
marginal statistics when estimating correlated predicates [6]. Learned
estimators likewise combine query features with sample evidence and show that
median accuracy can hide a much worse estimation tail [17]. Datalevin does not
claim sampling, Bayesian arithmetic, or shrinkage in isolation.

Robust query optimization explicitly considers uncertainty and the
performance/predictability tradeoff rather than selecting only for expected
cost [16]. Datalevin shares that objective but takes a storage-integrated path:
the same ordered structures provide current counts, rank-addressed samples,
directional execution, and the durable fanout target used to regularize a
query-local observation. The paper's empirical question is whether this small
mechanism is enough to reduce the plan tail created by raw sampling in a
complete durable system. Bitmap sideways-information passing follows the
broader family of pragmatic join filters [15].

## 6. Conclusion

Datalevin demonstrates that EAV flexibility need not preclude predictable
cost-based planning. Countable ordered indexes remove uncertainty about
marginal predicates; query-local samples address correlation and directional
fanout; and storage-prior shrinkage or a conservative envelope manages the
uncertainty introduced by those samples. Paired deterministic resampling
confirms the tail behavior: raw sampling produced a catastrophic
execution in 48 of 3,390 trials, while
storage-prior shrinkage reduced that count to eight and reduced P99 slowdown
from 12.26× to 2.64×. Exact counts separately prevented a broader tail,
including 30 timeouts when counts were hidden. The evidence supports layered
tail-risk reduction, not a claim that any estimator makes blowups impossible.
